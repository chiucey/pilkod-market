# Photo Collage — Vintage Editorial Style

One photograph → one collage poster. The photo is the star; the collage is its stage.
Not a filter, not a repainted illustration, not a full-bleed photo background.

## Workflow

```
1. Look at the photo yourself → write the dossier
2. Choose an anchor mode
3. Pull the palette
4. Choose elements + leave one free slot
5. Write the copy
6. Write the prompt and generate
7. Self-check → if it fails, use the failure table
```

Every later decision must grow out of the dossier. Skipping to the prompt degrades the output into a generic formula.

## 1 · Photo dossier

Look at the photograph and fill this in before anything else.

```
Subject:
Environment to cut out with it:  (rock / chair / railing / doorframe / bicycle…
                                  or "none, subject only")
Actually visible objects:        (list 5+, as specific as possible — not "plant"
                                  but "oleander")
Associable objects:              (only when the row above is thin. Must be things
                                  that would genuinely occur in THIS scene:
                                  rocky coast → barnacles, sea mist, salt crust,
                                  old mooring rope, gull tracks.
                                  NOT → compass, passport, plane)
Place / period cues:             (only if visible — never guess)
Light and time of day:
Mood (two or three words):
```

At least one decorative element must come from the "actually visible" row; the rest may come from "associable".
When the photo is sparse — just a person and the sea — richness comes from the associable row. Associate deeper into this scene, never outwards into the generic travel-symbol library.

## 2 · Invariants

1. **Never regenerate the subject.** Face, expression, pose, clothing, body proportions and skin tone must be the original photographic pixels. A dull composition beats a substituted person.
2. Cream textured paper ground with visible grain. Not pure white, not flat beige.
3. Four colours maximum including the ground. Two or three accents at 70–90% saturation.
4. No thin black outline frames, no sketchy pencil, no pure black — use deep navy or deep brown for dark passages.
5. Asymmetric, 35–50% negative space, subject off-centre.
6. Never invent a place name or date. Only if visible in the photo (street sign, landmark, wedding banner) or present in metadata.

## 3 · Craft standards

| Element | Pass mark |
|---|---|
| Watercolour | Genuine gradient (deeper one side, fading the other), edges bleeding into the paper fibres, darker where two washes overlap, visible granulation. Not a flat blob |
| Halftone | Individually visible dots in a regular screen, like silkscreen or old newsprint. Not noise, not a matte texture |
| Torn paper | Fibrous ragged edge with visible thickness. Not a clean cut |
| Cutout border | Thick, even, like real white card cut with scissors. Not a stroke filter |
| Display type | Italic at 8–12% of image height, confidently placed. Not small print in a corner |
| Engraving | Fine parallel hatching, antique book-plate density |

## 4 · Composition

1. **Bleed** — at least one element cropped by the canvas edge: a sun cut off at the top, a circle cut by a corner, a rock running off the bottom.
2. **Scale contrast** — largest element ≥50% of canvas width, smallest ≤5%, with intermediate steps.
3. **Interlock** — elements must overlap and pass through one another: the subject covering the anchor, line-art drawn across the subject and colour blocks, a tail or hem crossing a torn rectangle. Never let each element sit in its own tidy zone.
4. **Colour echo** — every accent colour appears at least twice, in separate regions.
5. **Negative space in one mass** — white space pools into one connected area rather than scattering through the gaps.
6. **Displaced weight** — push the subject to one side or corner, or let a large asymmetric block shove the balance off.

## 5 · The anchor

The anchor is the largest non-photographic shape in the frame; it pins the composition down. Three modes, chosen per photo:

| Mode | Use when | Form |
|---|---|---|
| A — Silhouette | The photo has a strong contour or sense of place (ridgeline, skyline, building, arch, tree canopy, wave, bridge) | That contour abstracted into a halftone silhouette, around 50% width |
| B — Mass | No legible contour (portrait close-up, interior, still life, open nature) | One huge watercolour mass, or a halftone circle or geometric shape; may bleed off |
| C — Subject as anchor | The subject is already large and graphic (sculpture, architecture, vehicle, big prop) | The subject anchors; any graphic shape drops to a smaller secondary role |

Do not manufacture a contour that isn't there. Forcing a ridgeline into a portrait close-up is worse than honestly using a mass anchor.
Suns and moons are one flavour of mode B; several consecutive images using one is laziness showing.

## 6 · Palette

Pull 2–3 accents from the photograph: one from the subject (clothing, skin, hair), one from the environment (sky, foliage, wall), one as counterweight (opposed in temperature or value to the first two). Push saturation to 70–90% — richer than the photo, never fully saturated.

## 7 · Elements

Four treatments, chosen by criterion:

| Treatment | Criterion | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| watercolour (default) | Soft, organic, layered surfaces: fabric, leather, foliage, water, fur, food, smoke | Bag, leaf, wave, flower, bread, shell |
| sticker | Read by outline, flat inside, must be legible instantly: implements, documents, vehicles, geometric symbols | Coffee cup, ticket stub, passport, compass, star, sunglasses |
| engraving | Antique objects and natural-history specimens; old-book-plate density | Compass, key, insect, bird plate, anchor |
| white line-art | Purely decorative, carries no narrative. Never the main element | Fern, wave, star, cloud, flowering stem, sparkle |

Objects outside the examples get classified by the same criteria — don't skip something just because it isn't listed.

**Vintage print ephemera** (optional, at most one per image): perforated stamp edge / ticket stub / newspaper clipping / handwritten tag / washi tape strip / circular postmark / library card / blueprint construction lines and registration marks / a block of pure halftone texture. The postmark is not mandatory; reach for it only when you want an archival feel.

**Line-art can be a field** — not necessarily three or four scattered motifs, but repeating rows across the full width (ranks of waves) or a scattered field (flowers and petals across the page). When the photo is sparse, a field beats icons.

**Two cutout modes**
- Clean cut with a thick white border: the outline cut precisely, on white card. Best when the pose and silhouette carry the image.
- Organic torn shape: the photo torn into an irregular shape retaining some background, with a ragged edge. Best when the background has texture worth keeping (foliage, blossom, patterned wall) and a hard cut-out would throw information away.

The subject may be split into multiple cutouts — the person as one piece, the sculpture they're touching as another — each with its own white border.

## 8 · Copy

Short poetry, a lyric fragment, or a keyword — never a caption. Take a phrase from the mood or moment, not a description of what's in the frame.

- Yes: `the morning` / `form & void` / `into the wild` / `wild rose` / `gldn hour`
- No: `woman standing on rock` / `beautiful sunset view` / `my trip 2024`

Up to three pieces; two is often enough:

| Piece | Content | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Display line | 2–4 words / a broken line of verse / a lyric fragment | Large italic serif or script, 8–12% of image height |
| Number | `no.3` / `NO. 9` / `NO. I` | Small caps or small serif; vary the casing between images |
| Small tag | A one-word footnote | Handwritten or typewriter face; may sit on washi tape or a label |

Type can sit on a colour block — cream lettering on a dark green torn rectangle — rather than always floating in the negative space.

## 9 · The free slot

Every image must contain one element that appears on none of the lists above, invented from the dossier. If nothing comes to mind, the dossier wasn't thorough enough — look at the photo again.

## 10 · Writing the prompt

The block below goes **verbatim at the very start of the prompt**. Do not reword it, do not shorten it:

```
CRITICAL — SUBJECT PRESERVATION: The subject must be the exact pixels from the
input photograph. Preserve the face, expression, skin tone, hair, clothing, body
proportions and pose EXACTLY as photographed. Do NOT regenerate, redraw,
re-render, beautify or substitute the person. Treat the subject as a physical
photo print that has been cut out with scissors and glued onto the page.
```

Compose the rest in your own words rather than filling a fixed skeleton, but make sure it specifies:

- Overall style: vintage 70s editorial analogue collage, zine aesthetic, watercolour and halftone, cream paper
- Subject: what it is, which environment objects come with it, which cutout mode, where it sits, whether an edge crops it
- Anchor: which mode, what shape, what colour, how large, where, whether it bleeds
- Watercolour behaviour: gradient direction, bleeding into paper fibres, darker where washes overlap, granulation
- Torn paper: colour, position, ragged edge, which element it overlaps
- Each element's treatment (watercolour / sticker / engraving / line-art), including the free slot
- Type: display line and its size ratio, number, tag
- Composition: asymmetry, how much negative space and where, bleed, interlock, colour echo, scale contrast
- The palette whitelist, plus no pure black / no black outline frames / no sketchy pencil / no border on the final image

## 11 · Self-check

```
[ ] Is the subject the original photo? Face / clothing / pose / skin tone intact?
[ ] Right anchor mode? No forced silhouette? Not another sun in a row?
[ ] Does something bleed off the edge?
[ ] Enough distance between largest and smallest?
[ ] Do elements overlap, or does each sit in its own zone?
[ ] Does every accent colour appear twice?
[ ] Real watercolour gradient? Visible halftone dots? Ragged torn edge?
[ ] Is the display line big enough?
[ ] At least one element from the photo's visible objects?
[ ] Is the free slot filled?
[ ] Four colours or fewer? No black frame? No invented place or date?
```

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Subject regenerated or replaced | The preservation block isn't first, or was reworded | Move it to the top, use it verbatim |
| Scattered, reads as a sticker tray | No interlock, no scale contrast | Blow one element past 50% and let the subject cover part of it; draw line-art across the subject |
| Flat, no presence | Nothing bleeds | Crop the anchor with the top edge or a corner |
| Every image alike | Elements came from the lists, not the photo | Redo the dossier; check whether the free slot was actually free |
| Watercolour reads as flat colour | Behaviour never specified | Add gradient direction, bleeding into paper fibres, darker where overlapping, granulation |
| Palette feels loose | Each accent used once | Repeat each colour in a second region |
| Weak type | No size ratio given | Add "8–12% of image height, visually prominent" |

If three attempts on the same image still fail, switch anchor mode and start over rather than patching the same composition.

## 12 · Output spec

3:4 portrait (default) or 4:3 landscape · 2K · JPG · no outer border on the finished image
