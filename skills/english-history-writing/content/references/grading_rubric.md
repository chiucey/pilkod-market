# Grading Rubric — English Academic History Writing
## Total: 100 Points

This document defines the grading standards for the `english-history-writing` skill's correction workflow.
Grade at **native English-speaker undergraduate academic standard** (equivalent to a first- or second-year university history essay at a UK/US institution).

---

## Overview of Dimensions

| # | Dimension | Max Points | Weight |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Thesis & Argument | 25 | 25% |
| 2 | Evidence & Analysis | 25 | 25% |
| 3 | Historical Knowledge | 20 | 20% |
| 4 | Language & Style | 20 | 20% |
| 5 | Structure & Coherence | 10 | 10% |
| | **Total** | **100** | **100%** |

---

## Dimension 1 — Thesis & Argument (25 points)

A history essay must advance a clear, defensible argument, not merely describe events.

### Band descriptors

| Band | Points | Description |
|---|---|---|
| Excellent | 23–25 | The essay opens with a focused, nuanced thesis that directly addresses the question. The argument is sustained and developed throughout; the conclusion reinforces and refines it. The essay goes beyond the obvious and engages with complexity or historiographical debate. |
| Good | 18–22 | A clear argument is present and maintained with reasonable consistency. Some nuance is evident. The essay occasionally drifts into description but returns to argument. |
| Satisfactory | 13–17 | An identifiable position exists but is vague, underdeveloped, or partially lost in descriptive passages. The conclusion may simply restate the introduction rather than synthesise the discussion. |
| Weak | 8–12 | Little discernible argument. The essay is largely descriptive or narrative. The question is addressed only superficially, or the response wanders off-topic. |
| Very Poor | 0–7 | No coherent argument. The essay may be entirely descriptive, or the thesis may contradict itself. The question is not meaningfully addressed. |

### Key markers to check
- Does the introduction contain a clear thesis statement?
- Does the body consistently support and develop that thesis?
- Does the conclusion synthesise — not merely repeat — the argument?
- Does the essay show awareness that historical questions are debatable?

---

## Dimension 2 — Evidence & Analysis (25 points)

Evidence must be relevant, specific, and interpreted — not simply listed or described.

### Band descriptors

| Band | Points | Description |
|---|---|---|
| Excellent | 23–25 | Specific, well-chosen historical evidence (dates, events, figures, statistics, sources) is used throughout. Every piece of evidence is clearly linked to the argument via explicit analysis. The student demonstrates the ability to weigh and evaluate evidence rather than accept it at face value. |
| Good | 18–22 | Evidence is relevant and generally specific. Analysis is present but occasionally descriptive. The link between evidence and argument is usually clear. |
| Satisfactory | 13–17 | Some relevant evidence is provided but it may be vague, imprecise, or inconsistently analysed. The student sometimes lists facts without drawing analytical conclusions. |
| Weak | 8–12 | Little relevant evidence. What is provided may be inaccurate, over-general, or used merely decoratively (i.e. quoted without analysis). |
| Very Poor | 0–7 | Virtually no supporting evidence, or evidence is fundamentally incorrect. The essay relies on unsubstantiated assertion. |

### Key markers to check
- Is evidence specific (named events, dates, people, places)?
- Does the student analyse rather than merely describe evidence?
- Is the evidence relevant to the specific argument being made?
- Is there over-reliance on a single source or narrow range of examples?

---

## Dimension 3 — Historical Knowledge (20 points)

The essay must demonstrate accurate and contextually appropriate historical knowledge.

### Band descriptors

| Band | Points | Description |
|---|---|---|
| Excellent | 18–20 | Factual content is consistently accurate and demonstrates broad and deep knowledge of the period/topic. The student situates events within their proper historical context. Where relevant, knowledge of historiographical perspectives (e.g. different scholarly interpretations) is demonstrated. |
| Good | 14–17 | Generally accurate knowledge with good contextual awareness. Minor factual errors or omissions that do not undermine the argument. |
| Satisfactory | 10–13 | Knowledge is sufficient to support a basic argument but shows gaps or inaccuracies. Some events or figures may be misidentified or mischaracterised. |
| Weak | 5–9 | Significant factual errors or shallow knowledge. Key events, dates, or figures are missing or incorrect. Limited contextual understanding. |
| Very Poor | 0–4 | Pervasive factual inaccuracies. The student shows little grasp of the historical period. |

### Key markers to check
- Are dates, names, and events accurate?
- Is the historical context correctly understood?
- Does the student demonstrate awareness of cause and effect, chronology, and change over time?
- Are any sweeping generalisations unsupported by knowledge?

---

## Dimension 4 — Language & Style (20 points)

Academic history writing requires formal register, precise vocabulary, clear sentence construction, and correct grammar.

### Sub-dimensions and allocation

| Sub-dimension | Max | Description |
|---|---|---|
| Grammar & Syntax | 8 | Correct use of tenses, subject-verb agreement, articles, prepositions, relative clauses, punctuation. No run-on sentences or sentence fragments. |
| Vocabulary & Precision | 6 | Accurate use of historical and academic vocabulary. Word choice is specific and precise; vague terms (e.g. "things", "got") and informal language are avoided. |
| Academic Register | 6 | Formal, analytical tone throughout. Avoids colloquialisms, contractions (e.g. "don't", "it's"), first-person opinion phrases without qualification ("I think"), and sensationalist language. |

### Band descriptors (overall Language & Style)

| Band | Points | Description |
|---|---|---|
| Excellent | 18–20 | Consistent academic register throughout. Prose is fluent, varied, and precise. Grammar and syntax are correct. Historical terminology is used accurately. |
| Good | 14–17 | Generally correct grammar and appropriate register. Occasional lapses in formality, word choice, or syntax that do not significantly impede reading. |
| Satisfactory | 10–13 | Noticeable errors in grammar, vocabulary, or register, but the meaning remains broadly clear. The writing may feel informal, repetitive, or imprecise. |
| Weak | 5–9 | Frequent grammatical errors that disrupt reading. Informal register, imprecise vocabulary, or poor sentence construction throughout. |
| Very Poor | 0–4 | Pervasive errors that make the essay difficult to understand. Register is entirely inappropriate for academic writing. |

### Common error categories (for inline annotation)

Use the following tags in inline comments:

| Tag | Type of Error | Example |
|---|---|---|
| `GRAMMAR` | Tense, agreement, articles, prepositions, punctuation | *"The revolution have started"* → *"The revolution started / had started"* |
| `VOCAB` | Wrong word, imprecise word, informal word | *"The war got bad"* → *"The conflict escalated significantly"* |
| `STYLE` | Informal register, first-person overuse, contractions, vague phrases | *"I think Napoleon was really great"* → *"Napoleon's domestic reforms had a profound impact…"* |
| `CONTENT` | Factual inaccuracy, unsupported claim, logical gap | *"The Battle of Waterloo was in 1816"* → *"The Battle of Waterloo was in 1815"* |
| `STRUCTURE` | Missing transition, abrupt shift, weak topic sentence | Add a transition connecting this paragraph to the previous one |

## Dimension 5 — Structure & Coherence (10 points)

A well-structured essay has a logical architecture: introduction, developed body paragraphs, and a conclusion. Each paragraph advances the argument.

### Band descriptors

| Band | Points | Description |
|---|---|---|
| Excellent | 9–10 | Clear three-part structure. Each body paragraph has a topic sentence, supporting evidence, and analytical commentary. Transitions between paragraphs are smooth and logical. The essay reads as a unified whole. |
| Good | 7–8 | Generally clear structure with effective paragraphing. Occasional weak transitions or paragraphs that are too short or too long. |
| Satisfactory | 5–6 | Basic structure is present but paragraphing may be inconsistent. Transitions are mechanical (e.g. "Firstly… Secondly… Finally…") or missing. Some paragraphs lack clear focus. |
| Weak | 3–4 | Poor organisation. Paragraphs are either absent or lack internal logic. The essay jumps between ideas without clear connection. |
| Very Poor | 0–2 | No discernible structure. The essay is a stream of consciousness or a single unbroken block of text. |

### Key markers to check
- Does the introduction provide context, narrow to a thesis, and signal the essay's structure?
- Does each body paragraph have a clear topic sentence?
- Are transitions between paragraphs logical and non-formulaic?
- Does the conclusion offer genuine synthesis (not just summary)?

## Scoring Guide: Letter-Grade Equivalents

| Total Score | Grade | Description |
|---|---|---|
| 90–100 | A+ / Distinction | Exceptional. Publishable quality for an undergraduate essay. Argument, evidence, knowledge, and language are all outstanding. |
| 80–89 | A / First Class | Excellent. Strong argument, detailed evidence, accurate knowledge, fluent academic prose. Minor room for improvement. |
| 70–79 | B / Upper Second | Good. Solid argument and knowledge with competent prose, but lacks the depth or precision for the highest grade. |
| 60–69 | C / Lower Second | Satisfactory. Some argument and evidence present, but significant weaknesses in one or more dimensions. |
| 50–59 | D / Third | Below expectations. Major weaknesses throughout, but a basic attempt at the task is evident. |
| Below 50 | F / Fail | Unsatisfactory. Fundamental weaknesses in most or all dimensions. Requires substantial revision. |

---

## Grading Procedure

1. **Read the entire essay once** without annotating to get an overall impression.
2. **Re-read paragraph by paragraph**, marking errors and strengths as inline comments.
3. **Score each dimension independently** using the band descriptors above.
4. **Sum the five dimension scores** for the final total out of 100.
5. **Write the Chinese commentary** (200–300 words) covering:
   - Overall impression and key strengths (1–2 specific positives)
   - Main issues (identify 3–5 specific problems, referencing the essay directly)
   - Targeted advice (concrete, actionable suggestions)
   - Closing remark (encouraging but honest; calibrate to the actual score)

### Tone calibration for commentary

| Score Range | Commentary Tone |
|---|---|
| 85–100 | Primarily affirming; highlight what makes the essay excellent; only minor suggestions |
| 70–84 | Balanced; acknowledge strengths clearly; specific guidance on what would take it to the next level |
| 55–69 | Constructive; direct about weaknesses; suggest concrete revision strategies; end encouragingly |
| Below 55 | Frank but supportive; prioritise the 2–3 most important areas to address; avoid overwhelming the student |

---

## Notes for Graders

- **Do not penalise non-native English speakers disproportionately** for Language & Style if their argument and knowledge are strong — Language & Style is only 20% of the total.
- **Do not reward impressive vocabulary that masks weak argument** — a sophisticated surface can disguise a lack of analytical depth.
- **Factual errors matter**: a single significant factual error (wrong date, wrong century, invented event) should cost at least 2–3 points in Dimension 3.
- **Source analysis essays** should be evaluated with extra attention to whether the student distinguishes between the content of the source and its perspective/limitations as evidence.
- **For very short essays** (clearly below the minimum word count): note this in the commentary and deduct up to 5 points from Dimension 5 (Structure), as an under-length essay almost certainly lacks development.
